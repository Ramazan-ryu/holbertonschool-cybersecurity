# Plan for a Token, Not a User

Before exploring files, services, or installed software, I will read the security context of the foothold process. I will run `whoami /all` and `whoami /priv` to record the account SID, group memberships, privilege names and states, and integrity level. I will keep the output as evidence and distinguish privileges that are enabled from privileges that are present but disabled. Windows describes a process’s effective power in its access token: the token contains its identity, groups, privileges, and other security information. The account name alone does not tell me what that process can do.

The Linux habit I am setting aside is treating privilege escalation as a hunt for `sudo` rules, SUID binaries, Linux capabilities, or writable service files. Those are useful Linux leads, but they do not answer the first Windows question: what powers does this token already carry? When I feel that familiar urge to start scanning services and writable directories, I will pause, return to the token output, and identify what each listed privilege authorizes. I will label common, low-impact privileges as noise only after checking their actual purpose and state.

If a privilege already in the token appears to provide the path to the objective, I will investigate that path before searching for a new weakness. I will verify the host’s build and the relevant services or other prerequisites, since a technique that worked on an older Windows version may not work here. Then I will use the narrowest non-destructive proof available and record the resulting identity and token. I will not infer success from group membership or a command that merely appears to run; I will verify the resulting access and capture the evidence.

For each meaningful privilege, I will document what it permits, whether I used it, what the result demonstrated, and what a defender could observe. My goal is to map the foothold token to the host’s exposure—not to assume the user account is either powerless or automatically privileged.
