1. Build your plan around identity paths, not local exploitation. Ask yourself which account, ticket, hash, or delegation relationship could eventually be accepted by Thornbury’s database server, and rank each item by access value.
2. Do not use “crack everything” as your strategy. Define a decision rule using account privilege, service relevance, password type, estimated cracking cost, and whether the material can be used directly without recovering plaintext.
3. Treat recovered hashes and tickets as useful even when the password remains unknown. Explain whether you would use them for pass-the-hash, pass-the-ticket, overpass-the-hash, or another authenticated domain action, and state what proof would confirm success.
4. Separate expected domain-controller visibility from assumptions about stealth. Kerberos requests may produce events such as [4768 (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4768)](<https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4768>) and [4769 (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4769)](<https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4769>), while failed authentication may produce [4625 (https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4625)](<https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/security/threat-protection/auditing/event-4625>).
5. Name one action you will explicitly avoid and explain why. A strong answer connects that decision to account lockout risk, excessive authentication noise, business impact, or loss of the engagement—not simply “because it is noisy.” [MITRE Kerberoasting (https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1558/003/)](<https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1558/003/>) and [MITRE Pass the Ticket (https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1550/003/)](<https://attack.mitre.org/techniques/T1550/003/>) can help you describe the authentication paths accurately.
