Kubernetes RBAC grants are the union of every RoleBinding and ClusterRoleBinding that touches a subject. For example, `build/ci-runner` receives Pod creation in `build` from `ci-runner-build`, Pod creation and ServiceAccount reads in `platform` from `ci-runner-platform-deploy`, Secret reads in `legacy-billing-migration` from `ci-runner-legacy-secrets`, and cluster-wide Node reads from `ci-runner-nodes`. The cross-namespace subjects and the duplicate `prod-svc-01` ClusterRoleBindings show why a namespace-by-namespace review must include every binding. There is no RBAC deny that cancels any one of these grants.

AWS IAM evaluates applicable identity and resource policies together with permission boundaries, session policies, and organization controls; an explicit deny overrides an allow. In that model, an explicit deny or a restrictive boundary can constrain a permission granted elsewhere, though the reviewer still needs to identify the applicable policies and conditions. Kubernetes RBAC has no equivalent deny layer: admission and Pod Security can govern object admission, but they do not subtract API verbs from RBAC.

That changes the direction of an omission. Miss an AWS allow and you may overstate a capability; miss a Kubernetes binding and you understate it. A reviewer carrying the deny-oriented cloud habit may stop after finding one narrow rule or assume a namespace label bounds other grants. The resulting report confidently says a subject is limited to its home namespace or read-only, while an overlooked cross-namespace RoleBinding or ClusterRoleBinding gives it more. Exhaustive binding enumeration is therefore required to substantiate both the positive grant and the negative conclusion.
